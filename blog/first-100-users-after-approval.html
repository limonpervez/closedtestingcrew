How to Get Your First 100 Users After Google Play Approves Your App

You cleared the 12-tester, 14-day closed testing requirement. Google granted Production access. Your app is live. And then, nothing. No downloads, no reviews, no signal that anyone besides your testers knows it exists. This is the part nobody warns first-time developers about: approval isn't launch, it's just the door opening. Here's a realistic plan for the first 100 real users, without a marketing budget.

Your closed testers aren't your growth engine

It's tempting to think the 12-20 people who tested your app are your first users. They're not, not in any way that matters for growth. They were recruited (or hired) specifically to satisfy a Google requirement, and most won't become long-term active users of an app outside their actual interests. Treat closed testing as a compliance step, and treat user acquisition as a completely separate problem that starts after approval.

Step 1: Your existing network, done right

Not a mass blast. A specific, personal message to the 10-20 people in your life who would genuinely use this app, explained in one sentence why it's useful to them specifically. This gets you your first handful of real, honest usage and feedback, which matters more at this stage than raw numbers.

Step 2: Store listing optimization, the free growth lever

Once you're live, your app's discoverability inside Google Play search itself is free, ongoing traffic if you get it right. Revisit your store listing specifically for search terms: does your short description include the actual words someone would type looking for an app like yours? Are your screenshots showing the specific value, not just generic UI? This single-digit-percent conversion lift compounds over every future visitor.

Step 3: Find the 2-3 communities where your exact user already hangs out

Not general app-promotion subreddits, which are mostly other developers, not users. The actual community around your app's use case: a hobby forum, a niche subreddit, a Discord server, a Facebook group. Read the room before posting. Most communities tolerate a genuine, non-spammy "I built this, here's why, happy to answer questions" post far better than a driveby link drop. Provide value in that community first if you can.

Step 4: Ask directly for reviews, honestly

Early reviews matter disproportionately for a new app's credibility and search ranking. After someone has genuinely used your app for a few days (not immediately on install), a simple, honest ask, "if this has been useful, a quick review would really help," converts better than an automated popup on day one. Never incentivize or manufacture reviews; Google's policy treats that the same as manipulated testing activity, and it risks the same kind of account trouble covered in what gets developer accounts suspended.

Step 5: Content that answers the question your app solves

If your app solves a specific problem, write about that problem. A short blog post, a video, a genuinely helpful forum answer that happens to mention your app as one solution among the ones you discuss. This is slower than a paid campaign but compounds over months in a way ads don't once you stop paying for them.

Step 6: Watch what actually converts, then double down

Track where your installs are actually coming from (Play Console's acquisition reports are a good free start). Most first-time developers are surprised which channel actually works, and it's rarely the one they expected. Once you see real signal, put more effort there and less into channels that aren't producing.

The realistic timeline

Ten real users in the first week is a reasonable, achievable target from personal network alone. Getting to 100 usually takes 3-6 weeks of consistent, small effort across the channels above, not a single viral moment.
